# Good Samaritan Shelter
## Making Community Support Easier to Find

*A nonprofit website redesign connecting user research, product requirements, and cross-functional delivery.*

**Role:** Product Management Co-lead  
**Organization:** Develop for Good × Good Samaritan Shelter  
**Timeline:** July–October 2025  
**Team:** 6-person cross-functional volunteer team  
**Focus:** User Research · Product Requirements · UX · Usability Testing · Launch & Handoff  
**Outcome:** Website launched and remains in use

---

## 01. Project Overview

Good Samaritan Shelter (GSS) is a nonprofit serving vulnerable communities across California's Central Coast through emergency shelter, housing assistance, recovery programs, employment support, and other services.

Our team partnered with GSS through Develop for Good to redesign its website, making community resources easier to find while creating a clearer experience for donors, community partners, and staff.

As a Product Management Co-lead, my primary contributions centered on **user research, synthesizing feedback, gathering and documenting requirements, and coordinating with the development team**. I also participated in the launch handoff and preparation of maintenance documentation for the client.

The project progressed from discovery and design through usability testing to a live website that GSS continues to use.

## 02. The Product Problem

GSS provides a wide range of essential services, but the existing website did not offer a straightforward way to discover and navigate them.

Service information was dispersed across the website, a centralized searchable directory was missing, and keeping program information current presented challenges for staff.

The redesign also needed to accommodate users with different goals.

| Audience | What they needed |
|---|---|
| People seeking assistance | Accessible service descriptions, locations, contact information, and eligibility details |
| Social workers and service navigators | Reliable information to help identify appropriate programs for clients |
| Donors and community partners | Clear organizational information, impact stories, and ways to contribute |
| GSS staff | A manageable way to maintain service information and website content |

**The central product question:**

How might we help visitors find essential community services more easily while supporting GSS's broader communication and content-management needs?

Our team needed to balance these different needs within the scope and resources available for a nonprofit redesign.

---

## 03. User Research: Finding the Real Priorities

One of my main responsibilities was supporting user research and translating findings into actionable product requirements.

Our survey collected **67 responses** and explored how people searched for community assistance, what information they prioritized, which devices they used, and how donors preferred to engage with the organization.

### Finding 1 — Visitors wanted actionable service information

Among 61 people answering a multiple-selection question about what they wanted to find quickly:

| Information | Responses |
|---|---:|
| Address and location | 54 |
| Phone number or email | 47 |
| Operating hours | 40 |
| Available services | 38 |
| Eligibility requirements | 33 |

The strongest signals were practical: visitors wanted to know where to go, how to make contact, and whether a program could meet their needs.

**Product implication:** Prioritize service discovery and structured, easy-to-find program details instead of relying primarily on broad descriptions of the organization.

### Finding 2 — Mobile-friendly access was important

Of the 59 people who answered the device-use question, **42 explicitly selected smartphones** as the device they most frequently used to search for help.

**Product implication:** Make responsive navigation and direct access to critical information core requirements.

### Finding 3 — Donors needed clarity and trust

Among 24 respondents to a donor-engagement question, frequently selected features included:

- Convenient donation pages — 16
- Impact reports and stories — 15
- Clear program descriptions — 14
- Contact information — 13

**Product implication:** Support a distinct donor journey without making the service-seeker experience harder to navigate.

### Translating the findings

I used research findings to help organize requirements around three priorities:

1. **Accessible help:** Put assistance pathways and essential service information within easy reach.
2. **Clear navigation:** Make important content understandable across devices and visitor types.
3. **Sustainable content:** Ensure staff could maintain useful, accurate information over time.

*Question-level sample sizes differ. Multiple-selection results represent selections, not mutually exclusive respondent groups.*

## 04. From Research to Product Requirements

Turning feedback into clear requirements was one of the most important parts of my role.

I contributed to the Product Requirements Document (PRD), which brought together stakeholder needs, user stories, acceptance criteria, functional priorities, UX requirements, and proposed success measures.

### Service discovery

The PRD defined a searchable directory that would allow visitors to identify relevant services through keywords and filtering options.

Individual service information needed to communicate essential details such as program descriptions, eligibility, location, contact information, and hours.

### Navigation and responsive design

The team defined consistent navigation and prominent entry points for visitors seeking help, exploring programs, learning about GSS, or donating.

Responsive design was included as a core requirement rather than treated as an optional enhancement.

### Content management

The PRD also considered the staff experience after launch.

Requirements included the ability for authorized administrators to manage service listings, update content, and publish organizational news.

This addressed a longer-term product concern: a site is only useful if its information can remain accurate and maintainable.

### Making requirements actionable

I helped translate research and stakeholder needs into documented user stories and acceptance criteria that designers and developers could work against.

For example, the need to keep service information current translated into requirements for staff to add, edit, and remove service records.

By making the intended functionality explicit, the PRD provided a shared reference point for decisions and cross-functional coordination.

## 05. Connecting Requirements to Design

The design team translated the requirements into wireframes for the website’s core pages and user journeys. My contribution was primarily product-focused: connecting research findings, stakeholder needs, and documented requirements with the proposed experience.

### Homepage — Clear paths for different audiences

The homepage wireframe shown above highlights **Get Help Now**, **Search Services**, and **Donate**, alongside the major service categories. Rather than expecting visitors to understand GSS's organizational structure first, these entry points help them choose an action based on why they came to the website.

**Product connection:** Survey respondents prioritized access to practical service information, while donor respondents emphasized convenient donation options. The homepage gives both audiences visible starting points.

**Product connection:** Address, contact details, operating hours, and eligibility were among the information types survey respondents most frequently wanted to find quickly. The proposed structure connects discovering a relevant service to knowing what action to take next.

**Product connection:** The survey highlighted convenient donation options, impact stories, and understandable program information. The proposed pages address those needs while keeping donor and volunteer actions distinct.

## 06. Prioritization and Tradeoffs

A key challenge was balancing usability, delivery effort, and the nonprofit's long-term maintenance needs.

The PRD documented proposed approaches including:

| Area | Documented approach |
|---|---|
| Frontend | React, Tailwind CSS, and DaisyUI |
| Service search | Fuse.js for flexible keyword search |
| Content administration | Retool-based administrative workflows |
| Deployment | Vercel |
| Project scope | A unified GSS website rather than separate sites for individual programs |

These were documented technical plans and considerations, not a claim that every proposed technology was ultimately used in production.

The product-level tradeoff was to prioritize a coherent, maintainable website that addressed essential user needs while reducing unnecessary implementation and operational complexity.

## 07. Usability Testing: Feedback on the Working Website

Before final delivery, our team invited **approximately 30 Good Samaritan Shelter staff members and visitors** to interact with the **working website**, rather than simply review static wireframes.

The hands-on sessions focused on two questions:

- Could participants use and navigate the redesigned website?
- Did they perceive the experience as an improvement over the previous website?

Participants gave positive feedback about the redesigned experience, and we did not receive specific feedback that led to documented design revisions from these sessions.

Involving both staff and visitors let the team hear from people who approached the website with different levels of familiarity with GSS's programs and operations. The feedback was encouraging ahead of the client handoff.

**Measurement limitation:** I do not have standardized satisfaction scores, task-completion rates, or a recorded before-and-after usability comparison. These sessions provide qualitative validation, not a quantified improvement claim.

## 08. Launch, Documentation & Client Handoff

The project extended beyond research and wireframes to an actual website launch.

As part of the delivery process, I supported the client handoff and helped prepare a **website maintenance guide** for Good Samaritan Shelter.

The guide was intended to make it easier for the client to maintain the website after the volunteer team's involvement ended.

This was especially important because GSS staff would need to manage evolving service information, organizational content, and future website updates.

The handoff reinforced a product principle I took away from the project: successful delivery is not only about launching functionality, but also about making that functionality sustainable for the people who inherit it.

**Final result:** The redesigned website launched and continues to be used by Good Samaritan Shelter.

## 09. Outcomes and Product Measurement

### What we delivered

Our cross-functional team delivered a launched nonprofit website redesign, supported by:

- Research findings from a 67-response survey
- Documented product requirements, user stories, and acceptance criteria
- Wireframes for the main site experiences, including service discovery and distinct supporter journeys
- Usability testing involving approximately 30 staff members and visitors
- Client handoff and maintenance documentation

These are documented project activities and deliverables, not post-launch performance metrics.

### How success was intended to be measured

The PRD proposed three indicators:

**Service directory engagement:** Monthly unique users of the service directory.

**Help-seeking actions:** Service referral or contact-link clicks.

**Donor engagement:** Online donation submissions.

These metrics reflected the product's intended outcomes for service seekers and donors.

I do not have verified post-launch analytics, so I have not assigned numerical improvements in adoption, conversion, or donations to the redesign.

## 10. Reflection

This project gave me hands-on experience connecting product discovery to actual delivery.

**Research should lead to decisions.** Survey results became more useful when I translated them into clearer priorities around service information, navigation, and mobile accessibility.

**Requirements create alignment.** A structured PRD helped connect nonprofit stakeholder needs with the work of designers and developers.

**Validation matters before delivery.** Having staff and visitors interact with the working website gave our team qualitative feedback before handoff, while reminding me to distinguish positive reactions from measured task success.

**Product ownership continues through handoff.** The maintenance guide and client transition showed me why long-term usability and operational continuity matter as much as initial launch.

Most importantly, the project strengthened my ability to organize ambiguous needs into actionable requirements and collaborate across functions to deliver a digital product used by a real organization.
